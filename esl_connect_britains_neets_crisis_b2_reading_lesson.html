Britain's NEETs Crisis

B2 reading lesson | Young people, unemployment and opportunities

Warm-up questions

  1. Why can finding a first job be more difficult than finding a second one?
  2. What kinds of support might help young people move into work or training?
  3. Do you think employers should offer jobs to people with little experience? Why?

Lesson aims

Explore why some young people in Britain are not in education, employment or training. Practise reading for details and comparing experiences, employment vocabulary, conditionals and giving balanced opinions.

Reading text

Adapted from the BBC report for B2 learners. The individuals' experiences and reported figures are drawn from the original article.

In Britain, around one million people aged 16 to 24 are not in education, employment or training. They are often described as NEETs. A report by former government minister Alan Milburn calls the situation both an economic and a moral crisis. But behind this general label are very different personal stories.

Michael, 19, from the Wirral, left college after his social anxiety became difficult to manage. At first, he stayed at home and felt disconnected from education and work. He found it hard to get a job because employers wanted experience, yet he could not gain experience without being hired. With help from the Merseyside Youth Association, he began work experience in a supermarket and later worked shifts in a community café. He now feels much more confident and hopes to find steady employment.

Maariyah, 22, has a university degree in history and politics and dreams of working in museums. Nevertheless, she is unemployed after finishing a temporary contract. She spends hours at the library sending at least 20 carefully prepared job applications each week. She has even been rejected from unpaid voluntary positions. She worries that employers may use AI to screen applications before a person has read them. Maariyah wants employers to give enthusiastic young applicants a chance.

Matthieu, 19, from north-west London, is autistic and volunteers at a gardening club and a computer repair shop. A supported internship in a hospital helped him feel more comfortable talking to people. He would like practical work in a factory, warehouse or delivery role, but missing maths and English GCSE qualifications is an obstacle. He says that employers may need to give him more time to learn, but they could be surprised by what he can achieve.

Alex, 20, from St Helens, was studying animal management before anxiety and exhaustion led her to leave college. She also helps care for her mother. Alex has been looking for a job for a year and takes part in an employment-coaching programme. She says many local businesses have closed or moved away, making it harder to find work. Although she would love to work with animals, she applies for cleaning and retail jobs too.

The young people also described the importance of financial support. Michael and Alex receive Universal Credit while they look for work. Alex worries that cutting benefits could make life more difficult for young people who still have no job. The report considers changes to welfare support and ways of encouraging employers to hire young people.

The problem is not simply a lack of ambition. Some young people face mental-health difficulties, disabilities or missing qualifications. Others are highly qualified but cannot find a first opportunity. The BBC reports that about one in five NEETs had a mental-health condition, according to 2025 Department for Education data; it also notes that around one in ten NEETs are university graduates.

Employers also face difficulties. Some businesses told the BBC that higher costs, including wages and energy bills, have made it harder to create new jobs. For young people, this makes a competitive job market even more challenging. However, the four interviewees believe that mentoring, training and employers willing to provide a first chance can make a difference.

Their experiences show that the term NEET covers people with different qualifications, needs and ambitions rather than one single group. The central question is how education providers, employers and public services can work together to help more young people build a future.

Reading Comprehension

Answer in your own words, then click Reveal answer. Click Highlight evidence to see the relevant phrase in the text.

1. Why was Michael caught in a difficult cycle when he tried to find work?
He needed experience to get hired, but he could not gain that experience without a job.
2. How does Maariyah show that she is actively looking for work?
She goes to the library and submits at least 20 tailored applications each week.
3. What barriers does Matthieu face, and what kind of work would he like?
He lacks some GCSE qualifications and needs time to learn; he is interested in practical jobs such as warehouse, factory or delivery work.
4. What local problem makes Alex's job search harder?
Businesses in her area have shut down or moved away, so there are fewer opportunities.
5. Why is Alex worried about possible cuts to benefits?
Because benefits provide financial support while she looks for a job, and cuts could make life much harder.
6. What two statistics show that NEETs do not all face the same situation?
About one in five had a mental-health condition in the cited data, while about one in ten are university graduates.
7. What is the central message about the label “NEET”?
It describes people with many different circumstances and should not be treated as one uniform group.

Discussion

1. First jobs

Should employers provide more entry-level jobs that do not require previous experience?

2. Individual circumstances

Which barriers faced by Michael, Maariyah, Matthieu and Alex are most difficult to overcome? Explain your reasoning.

3. Technology

How can AI make job applications more efficient, and how might it create problems for applicants?

4. Support systems

What roles should schools, employers, mentors and government services play in helping young people find work?

Speaking challenge

Imagine that you are advising a local youth centre. Propose three practical initiatives to improve opportunities. Include at least two first or second conditional sentences.

Writing challenge

Write 140–180 words: “Is work experience more important than qualifications for getting a first job?” Give a balanced argument and examples from the reading.
